NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 · Lunar Landing Site Navigator

At the Moon's south pole, the Sun never gets off the horizon.

It circles the sky at most 1.5° up, so a single ridge decides whether a lander has power, and a low Earth decides whether it can talk home. This tool turns NASA's terrain and ephemeris data into one answer: where to land, when, and what to do if the day goes wrong.

Why a landing site is a date as much as a place

On Earth, noon comes every day. At 89°S on the Moon, daylight depends on the local skyline, the season and the day of the month. Move the landing by 200 metres or two weeks and the same mission goes from weeks of sunlight to days of darkness.

1.54°axial tilt

Light comes in sideways

The Sun never climbs higher than about 1.5° above a flat polar horizon, so shadows are tens of kilometres long and a nearby rim can block it for days.

±6.7°libration

Earth can set

Near the pole the Earth sits on the horizon too. It rises and sets with the Moon's monthly wobble, and when it is down there is no direct radio link.

14days of slip

Schedules move

Launches slip. A site that is perfect on the planned date can be in the dark two weeks later, so every answer here is shown against a slip.

How the algorithms decide

Three small models of what the app does on real data. They use toy terrain so you can see every step; the app runs the same logic on LOLA elevation and the Horizons ephemeris.

1 · Is the Sun up? The skyline test

From the site, look along the Sun's direction and find the highest ridge angle. The site is lit only if the Sun is higher than that. Drag the site and the Sun.

Vertical scale exaggerated. The app repeats this test for 360 directions around the site, every hour of the stay, for the Sun and for the Earth.

2 · Where to land: a two-stage screen

Stage 1 checks hundreds of grid points cheaply against hard limits, then scores what is left. Stage 2 spends its effort on the eight best: fine terrain inside the landing ellipse and 2,000 simulated touchdowns each. Five are kept.

  1. Hard limits: slope, permanent shadow, longest dark spell
  2. Score the rest: Sun, Earth, slope, distance to shadow
  3. Take the eight best into detail; simulate touchdowns
  4. Re-rank on the fine figures; keep five
too steeppermanent shadowdark longer than the battery lastssuitability scoretaken into detail

Default weights are the app's: Sun 40, Earth 30, slope 20, distance to shadow 10. In the app, each spot also gets a slip-robustness figure: the share of late landing days on which it still passes.

3 · Rover route: A* under slope limits

The route search spreads out from the start over the terrain grid, never crossing ground steeper than the rover's limit, until it reaches the goal. Lower the limit and watch the route detour, or fail and say what limit it would need.

steeper than the limitground the search looked atchosen route

The app searches the 240 m LOLA grid with separate climb, descent and cross-slope limits, returns shortest, flattest and sunniest candidates, then simulates the battery along the chosen one.

In flight: what does the lander do?

Event
Phase of flight

On the surface: the nearest safe haven

Every 2 km along the drive the app finds the closest place that is sunlit for the next day, has the Earth in view, and can be reached on the battery left. Move along the route.

Launch slips

The Map re-scores the landing for every day of slip up to 14 days and names the cheapest way out: another date, or a spot nearby that stays lit.

Radio goes quiet

The rover gets a heading to its haven given by the Sun and the Earth in the sky, so it can hold course without a link.

Hardware changes

Vehicle mass, engine, battery and rover limits are assumptions you can edit; Finalise re-checks go / no-go before anything is frozen.
